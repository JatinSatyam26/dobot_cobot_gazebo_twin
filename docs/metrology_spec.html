Read this before you pick up a tape measure

Four things in the photographs overturned the earlier plan

1. The M1 Pro does not use vacuum. Its tool is a passive blue 3D-printed slotted fork blade. The wafer rests on it under gravity alone — no cup, no vacuum line, no suction. Only the Pro 600 has a suction cup.

2. That changes the pick motion, not just a parameter. A cup descends vertically and seals. A fork must be inserted horizontally beneath the wafer and then lifted. Approach direction, clearance under the wafer, and fork blade thickness all become first-class requirements that a vacuum cell never has.

3. There are no photoelectric sensors. The belt is stepper-driven and the PLC runs it purely on time. Belt speed accuracy therefore is position accuracy — the opposite of a sensor-triggered cell.

4. The belt makes a three-part move, not one. Point A (load) → run → Point B, mid-journey dwell of 5–10 s → run → Point C (unload). The dwell is part of the cycle and must be in the sequencer.

“100% real-to-sim” is not the goal, and chasing it will cost you the deadline

A simulation cannot match reality exactly — belt slip, stepper microstep error, joint backlash, and the wafer shifting on the fork are all real and none are in your model. The achievable and genuinely useful target is different: the sim should predict the same outcomes as the cell. Does the fork get under the wafer? Does anything collide? Is the cycle time right to within a few percent? Get those three and you have a legitimate digital twin.

You do not need absolute positions on the table

Where the cell sits on the lab table is cosmetic. What determines whether the sim behaves like the cell is a chain of relative transforms between stations:

  1. M1 Pro base → yellow nest (pick)
  2. M1 Pro base → magenta holder at Point A (place)
  3. Point A → Point B → Point C (belt travel, in time)
  4. Pro 600 base → magenta holder at Point C (pick)
  5. Pro 600 base → blue nest (place)

Get those right and the twin is right even if the whole board is 50 mm from where you think it is on the lab table. Spend your measuring time here.

02

Datum and frame convention

Nothing else means anything until a datum exists. The datum belongs on the wooden board, because the board is what the three stations share — the lab table underneath it is height-adjustable and irrelevant.

03

Board and robot mounting poses

Six numbers place each robot. Five are easy; yaw is the one that quietly ruins everything, because angular error multiplies by reach.

04

Conveyor geometry and stepper drive

Because the belt is stepper-driven with no encoder and no sensor, you can calculate its speed far more precisely than you can time it — provided you capture the drive parameters below.

Compute belt speed, don't just stopwatch it

With a stepper coupled directly to the end roller:

mm_per_step = (π × D_roller) / (steps_per_rev × microsteps)
belt_speed = mm_per_step × step_frequency

Capture all four inputs and you get belt speed to a fraction of a percent. Then stopwatch it once over ≥500 mm as a sanity check — if measured and calculated disagree by more than a few percent, the belt is slipping on the roller, which is itself something the sim should know about.

05

Wafer

The wafer is the moving part the whole cell exists to handle, and it has a primary flat — which means its orientation is a real state variable, not just its position.

06

Stepped nests and the belt holder

All three fixtures share a geometry: concentric semicircular terraces with an open chord side. The terraces centre the wafer; the open side is fork clearance. Measure one carefully and the others go quickly.

The single most likely physical failure in this cell

The fork does not reach under the wafer inside the nest — it slides under the portion that overhangs the open chord side. So the governing inequality is:

wafer_underside_height − surface_below_overhang > fork_thickness + clearance

At the yellow and blue nests the surface below the overhang is the wooden board. On the conveyor it is the belt, and that is the tight one: the magenta holder is short, so the gap between the wafer's underside and the belt may be only a few millimetres. If the fork blade is thicker than that gap, the cell physically cannot work — and no amount of tuning the sim will reveal it unless you have measured both numbers.

07

End effectors

The two robots pick in fundamentally different ways — a passive fork lifting from below, a vacuum cup pressing from above. Both tools are now fully visible in the photographs, so what follows is dimensioning a known shape rather than discovering one.

08

PLC sequence and timing

With no sensors, the PLC's clock is the cell's position feedback. Every duration below is load-bearing — get one wrong and the wafer stops in the wrong place.

Setting your budget

Two capture tolerances matter, and they are different for the two robots. For the Pro 600's cup, the budget is how far off-centre it can land and still seal — generous on a large flat wafer. For the M1 Pro's fork, the budget is set by the nest's open chord width and the fork's width: the blade has to enter the gap without clipping the nest walls, and it has to end up far enough under the wafer's centre of mass that the wafer does not tip when lifted.

Read the table one way: base yaw and belt speed dominate. If you have time to measure exactly three things well, make them the two robot yaw angles and the stepper drive parameters.

10

The shortcut that beats measuring

Use the robots as their own measuring machines

Jog each real robot to each waypoint — nest pick, fork-insert, lift, conveyor place, retreat — and record the joint values off the pendant or DobotStudio. Those numbers are already in the robot's own frame, which is exactly the frame the sim controller commands in.

Replay the recorded joint values in Gazebo and every error in your world model cancels out of the arm's motion: you are no longer asking the sim to compute where the nest is.

This matters more for a fork than for a cup. A fork pick is a multi-segment move — approach, insert horizontally, lift, retract — and each intermediate pose has to clear the nest walls. Teaching those poses on the real robot captures clearances you would otherwise have to derive from fixture geometry you have not measured yet.

Two honest limits. It works because the model's kinematics come from Dobot's CAD — a wrong link length would still corrupt the replay. And it validates the motion, not the environment: for reach envelopes and collisions you still need sections 02–06.
